1000 Flavors Chocolate Kiosk - ED Workshop

What if buying chocolate felt more like making it? For an Experience Design workshop, my group designed a self-service kiosk for chocolate shops in French-Speaking part of Switzerland that lets customers build a personalized recipe, pay on the spot, and watch their creation being prepared live in the store.

Brief:

User Journey Mapping:

The first step was mapping the user flow to establish a clear and intuitive journey. Alongside structuring the flow, we analyzed the design decisions required at each stage, carefully evaluating how users interact with the interface and what influences their choices. We also considered the cognitive and emotional responses triggered at every decision point to ensure the experience felt seamless, guided, and psychologically aligned with user behavior.

Using Mascot:

Our core concept was to introduce a persistent on-screen mascot that acts as a supportive guide throughout the experience. The mascot is designed to step in whenever users feel overwhelmed or uncertain, offering contextual assistance and reassurance without disrupting the flow.

Translating user flow into wireframes

The primary challenge we addressed was the sheer volume of choices presented to users. With so many options available, the risk of decision fatigue was high. The mascot helped reduce cognitive overload by simplifying complex moments and providing gentle guidance at key decision points.

Additional screens allow users to select their preferred spice profiles, which are visually grouped and positioned along an axis to communicate relationships and intensity levels. This layout helps users compare options more intuitively and make informed choices.
For ingredient selection, we implemented a hierarchical tree structure. This approach organizes information in a clear, layered format, enabling users to explore details progressively without feeling overwhelmed.

To streamline the selection process for choosing the cacao’s country of origin, we structured the experience to prevent information overload. Instead of displaying all 20 countries on a single screen, we grouped them by their respective five continents. This approach reduced visual clutter, made navigation more intuitive, and allowed users to process their options in manageable steps.
